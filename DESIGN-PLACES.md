# Places, commodities and demand (design, 2026-10-28)

Jeff's direction: the whole country has people in it and they all need goods; every place
produces something, even if only in season; trucks should be seen in Idaho, Montana, Wyoming
and the Fort Myers area, not just on the interstates between the 60 metros. Loads should carry
a commodity, because a commodity brings its own urgency: produce can't sit on the board, dry
retail can. This step comes before shipper leverage and contracts, which will be computed from
the flows it creates.

## What stays and what changes

- The **60 hubs stay as markets**: load boards, the load-to-truck ratio, reference rates,
  home hubs, yards, weigh-station pull-in rates and the AI companies all keep working per hub.
- **Places** are a second tier under the markets: every metro and micropolitan area plus rural
  anchors so that nowhere in the lower 48 is more than about 75 miles from one. Each place
  belongs to the nearest market and sits on the road graph like a hub.
- Every **load** gets an origin place, a destination place and a **commodity**. Its miles are
  the road route between the places; its market (for the board and the rate) is the origin
  place's market; its rate per mile is the market lane's reference, applied to the real miles.
- Every **truck** sits at a place. Empty miles to a pickup are place to place. Repositioning
  still targets markets (their own place).
- Each commodity brings **equipment, value, an appointment window and spoilage**: produce posts
  with a delivery appointment a day or two out, climbs in price every round it sits, spoils when
  late or when the reefer fails, and brings a cargo claim; frozen and other reefer freight is a
  notch less urgent; dry retail and industrial freight can roll for days; steel and lumber
  longer still. Hot loads become a property of the commodity and the appointment.

## Data (packages/data)

- `places.ts` (generated by `tools/build-places.mjs` from GeoNames cities500, CC BY 4.0; Natural
  Earth's populated places stop at 679 towns for the whole country, GeoNames has 21,785): id, name,
  state, lat, lon, population, market index, and the point where the town joins the road. Selection
  (4,786 places): every town over 10,000 people (a suburb within 5 miles folds into its neighbour,
  its people counted; a county seat of about the same size keeps its name), every county seat and
  state capital over 500, every hub, then a coverage pass over a half-degree grid that adds the
  largest remaining town of 1,000+ in any cell without one. Every town joins the road beside it:
  the road network splits there, so a route between two towns runs through the towns between them
  on the real highway (a town on US-93 is on US-93, not at the next junction seventy miles on).
  The builder looks at the nearest spot on every road within 40 miles and takes the nearest one
  that isn't a real detour to a hub, because Natural Earth's roads have loose ends (Castle Rock's
  nearest road ran 209 miles to Denver, 26 miles away, before the road build learned to tie them).
  The market is the hub reached soonest by road.
- `commodities.ts`: about 25 commodities with equipment, value per load, appointment window,
  spoilage rule, weight class and a seasonal profile (national or regional).
- `economy.ts`: production and consumption by place. Consumption follows population (grocery,
  retail, fuel, building materials, parcels). Production comes from a regional economy table,
  state defaults with place overrides: Idaho potatoes, dairy and timber; Montana wheat, cattle and
  oil in the east, timber in the west; Wyoming coal, oil and beef; South Florida and the Rio Grande
  Valley produce October to May; the Central Valley produce, nuts and dairy; the Corn Belt grain,
  meat packing and machinery; Permian and Bakken oilfield freight; the Carolinas furniture and
  poultry; Georgia poultry and carpet; the ports (LA/Long Beach, NY/NJ, Savannah, Houston,
  Seattle/Tacoma, Oakland, Norfolk, Charleston, Miami, Baltimore) imports; Detroit, Ohio and
  Indiana autos and parts; Pittsburgh, Gary and Birmingham steel; the Gulf Coast chemicals and
  plastics; Wisconsin, Minnesota, Idaho, California and New York dairy; Oregon and North Carolina
  Christmas trees in November. Public sources to calibrate against later: USDA NASS county
  production, BLS QCEW employment by industry, FAF5 commodity flows.
- `flows.ts`: a gravity model. For each origin place and commodity, the share of its production
  that goes to each destination place is consumption there times exp(−distance/λ), with λ by
  commodity (produce travels; building materials don't). The national volume of each market stays
  what the current demand plan gives it, so the economy's scale doesn't move; the plan's loads are
  spread over the market's places by production share and sent by the gravity model.

## Engine

- `Load`: `commodity`, `originPlace`, `destPlace`, `appointmentT`, `spoilsAfterMin`; `origin`
  and `dest` stay the markets. `dueT` becomes the earlier of the HOS-based transit window and the
  appointment. Late past a commodity's grace: the usual late penalty, plus for perishables a
  spoilage claim (a share of cargo value on the carrier) and the load counted as a service failure.
- `Truck`: `place` (current or leg origin). `hubRoute` gains `placeRoute` (road routing between
  place attachments, cached by pair). Trips, weigh stations, tolls and the map use the place route.
- Boards: a load's board is its origin market; the board shows place names ("Homestead, FL →
  Hunts Point, NY · produce"). Dispatchers see deadhead from the truck's place.
- Reefer breakdowns spoil perishables after a commodity-specific number of hours.
- Bots: no new logic in this step; their orders already work per market.

## Client

- Map: places drawn as small dots sized by population; trucks move along place routes, so the
  US highways through Montana finally carry traffic.
- Load board: commodity, places, appointment ("deliver by Tue 14:00"), spoilage warning.
- Guide: a page on commodities and appointments.
